AI writes the code and checks it. Fine. But who is responsible?

AI can now write the code and check it within a strict process. One question remains: who is responsible for it once the code is in production?

Part of a series of articles on AI applied to code.
The previous article, on checking the code: “AI writes the code. Fine. But who checks it?”

The code is in prod. Who answers for it?

The previous article stops at code validation: code written, then checked, in a strict process, by several AIs from different vendors. Then everything follows: the pull request (PR) is merged, the code goes to production, and customers use it every day. And one night, at 3 a.m., production goes down and an alert fires.

In a well-run organization, roles are clear: the alert goes to the support team, on call. Watching prod and stepping in at night is its job. It stabilizes production. During the day, the developer takes over: they know their code well, and they help support understand and fix. They are woken up in the middle of the night only in an absolute emergency.

Support answers for production, the developer answers for their code.

Now AI writes the code, sometimes with no human involved: there is no longer necessarily a developer who understands it. At best, a person who writes a prompt, sometimes even a bot that fires the request. So who is responsible for the code? Who does support turn to for a fix?

V-model or Agile, the same steps!

To understand who is responsible for what, we distinguish eight steps, from the initial idea through to production:

  1. Why? The goal, the will, the reason for doing it, the problem that justifies the work.
  2. What? The specification, what the software must do, the expected result.
  3. How? The design, the way to build it.
  4. Build! The development, writing the code.
  5. Test! Unit tests then integration tests, to make sure the code works.
  6. Verify! Review, prove, look for what will break, secure. The last line of defense.
  7. Deploy! The deployment, to put the application update into production.
  8. Monitor! Once in production, check that everything goes as expected, alert when something goes wrong.

This framework helps clarify each person’s role. Depending on the method chosen, these steps follow one another, overlap or repeat.

First the V-model, which runs a project in a single pass. The descending branch of the V refines the need, from specification down to detailed design. The ascending branch tests in reverse order, from unit tests up to acceptance, and each level of testing answers a design step. Everything is planned up front, and a specification error often shows up only at the end, months later, when fixing it costs the most.

Then Agile, which answers the V-model’s flaw with an iterative cycle: short loops, a few weeks at most. On each lap, the team ships a small part of the software, tests it, adjusts course and fixes errors as early as possible.

These two approaches, which seem opposed in every way, nevertheless go through the same steps. And with AI, we are still, for now, in this same cycle.

The dividing line between human and AI

AI can now do everything, fine. But it is up to the human to decide what they want to build! The human is in command and must stay there. In this cycle, the human sets the “Why?”, the goal, and still today should (must?) define the “What?”, the expected result.

Take a simple example. Earning a living is a “Why?”: it says nothing yet about the road. Starting a company or looking for a job are two “What?”, two very different paths to the same goal. This idea of the “Why?” comes from Simon Sinek: in his famous talk “Start with why”, one of TED’s most watched, he makes it the starting point of everything. Apple is the flagship example there: its “Why?”, challenging the status quo and thinking differently, the famous “Think different”, has taken the shape, in turn, of a computer, a music player, a phone and a tablet. One “Why?” opens up several “What?”. Choosing which one is the decision that commits, and it too belongs to the human.

In a few months, AI has made considerable progress, and it now knows how to design, code, test and deploy. It can start taking care of everything from the “How?” onward, right to the end of the cycle. That is fine: these steps are “only” the execution of human will.

This line is not fixed: it is a slider. The human sometimes moves it, to keep a hand on the design. Tomorrow, AGI (artificial general intelligence, at human level) could push it the other way, and also choose what to build. Even on that day, the “Why?” must stay in our hands: deciding what is worth doing is what makes us human.

When a developer drives the AI and ships the code, nothing changes: they answer for it, as before. Without a developer, whoever defines the “What?” is, by default, responsible for the code delivered and for what runs in production.

When something goes wrong, there is a very handy phrase for dodging blame: “The AI did it.” No. Being a professional means precisely answering for your work. A professional does not hide behind their tools. AI does the work, it does not answer for it.

A lawyer would say exactly the same thing, by the way. In 2024, Air Canada argued that its chatbot alone was responsible for its answers: the tribunal ruled against the airline. No court will summon an AI: it will look for the person, or the company, that decided to use it.

The human is responsible, never the AI.

The trap: deciding without being able to fix

Picture someone in marketing who asks for a feature: they define the “What?”, and should therefore carry the responsibility for it. Yet they do not know the code, do not understand it, and can do nothing when production goes down. Behind them, nobody takes over. The “What?” has an author, the code has no one.

Asking for a feature does not mean knowing how to fix it, but it does mean planning who can step in. So someone responsible for the code, an “owner”, must be named from the start, for each feature. In practice: a name in every ticket, before launching the AI.

If that role requires technical skills the author of the “What?” does not have, another person must be named, a developer or a team. That does not let the author of the “What?” off the hook: they answer for what they asked for, the named person answers for the code. That is who support turns to, the next morning. They can of course lean on AI to understand and fix. But they are the one who decides, and who answers for it.

Conclusion

The human defines the “Why?” and the “What?”, AI then executes. But then, who is responsible? For us, a human, always! Named from the start, they decide and supervise the AI.

So at 3 a.m., when production goes down, support knows who to turn to: a named person who understands the code and has the means to act.

AI executes, but the will remains human.
